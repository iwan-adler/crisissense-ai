      How LooMap works

      1. Collect. For the chosen area LooMap searches the Landsat 8/9 Collection-2 archive (USGS, via Microsoft Planetary Computer) for the clearest scenes of the last 3 years and downloads thermal (land-surface temperature) and optical bands. Clouds and shadows are removed with the QA band.
      2. Engineer. Each 500 m block gets vegetation (NDVI), built-up (NDBI) and open-water share from the same scene, road density and distance to water and green space from OpenStreetMap, relative elevation (Copernicus DEM) and the ERA5 air temperature, humidity and wind at the moment the satellite passed.
      3. Learn. Two parts. A city-level model predicts the day’s average surface temperature from air temperature (plus seasonal sun angle when that tests better on left-out dates). A gradient-boosted tree model, written from scratch and trained in your browser, learns how much hotter or cooler each block is than that average. Monotone constraints keep it physically sensible: more vegetation or water never raises predicted heat, and more built-up surface never lowers it.
      4. Prove. Spatial 4-fold cross-validation holds out whole neighbourhoods and compares against a same-day city-average baseline, a linear model and a greenery-only model. Quantile models give a P10–P90 band, recalibrated (split-conformal) so it really covers ~80% of held-out observations.
      5. Forecast. Each block’s latest surface state is combined with the Open-Meteo forecast for the next 3 days at satellite overpass time. Feels-like risk = city forecast apparent max + 0.3 × the block’s surface-heat anomaly (an assumed surface-to-air coupling), banded with US NWS heat-index categories.
      6. Explain & act. Every block shows the features pushing its temperature up or down (tree-path attribution), Getis-Ord Gi* marks statistically significant hotspots, and the what-if tool re-runs the model with more trees or new development.

      Limits — read before using

      • A research prototype, not a heat alert or a health warning.
      • Landsat measures surface temperature at ~10:30 local time, not the air temperature people feel or night-time heat.
      • OpenStreetMap is less complete in informal settlements, which are often the most heat-vulnerable.
      • What-if scenarios change satellite indices, not real materials; treat them as directional.

      Data: USGS Landsat 8/9 C2 L2 · Microsoft Planetary Computer · © OpenStreetMap contributors · Open-Meteo (ERA5, ECMWF/DWD/NOAA forecasts, Copernicus DEM). No personal data is used. Nothing leaves your browser except requests to these public APIs.
